The platform started remembering your guests

Until now every conversation with a hotel began from zero. A guest who told reception last March that they are coeliac told them again in September. A guest who ordered the same gin and tonic on four evenings was a stranger on the fifth. The hotel held the information — scattered across orders, requests, surveys, chat transcripts and a receptionist's memory — but nothing joined it up. This fortnight the platform learned to.

Every guest now has one memory: a list of plain-sentence facts about them, each one dated, each one carrying where it came from. Some facts are collected mechanically from what the guest actually did — orders, reservations, room requests, interests they ticked, survey answers, data from the hotel's own booking system. The genuinely new part that landed this cycle is that the platform also reads finished conversations. When a chat with reception or with the AI assistant ends, the system re-reads it once and writes down what it learned, keeping the guest's own words as evidence beside every fact.

That memory is then put to work in three places at once. Reception and managers get a Guest memory panel and a one-line summary in the admin panel; the crew app shows a pre-arrival brief before the guest walks in. The AI concierge reads the most relevant facts before answering, so it stops giving a regular visitor the newcomer's answer. And a Recommended for you row now appears on the guest app home screen and the in-room TV, built from what that guest has shown they like.

It was built carefully, because it had to be. Facts the system merely inferred — as opposed to things the guest stated or did — never reach the guest; they stay staff-only. Categories that would touch health, religion, politics or ethnicity do not exist in the system at all; an allergy is recorded as a service requirement, which is what the kitchen actually needs to know. Staff can correct or delete any fact, every staff view is access-logged, and the guest-facing half stays switched off for a hotel until that hotel's consent terms are in place.

Hotel onboarding

Building a hotel from its own website grew up

Admin panelNevron opsIn QA

A hotel built from its website, now fit to show a customer

Nevron can point an automated reader at a hotel's existing website and have a complete, ready-to-show hotel account come out the other end — name, logo, colours, photographs, menus, a guest home screen, a QR code. It worked, but on a fleet of hotels built that way ten separate faults were found and fixed this fortnight: headings that came out unreadable because the website's own colour was too pale, dish prices that vanished when the price had no currency symbol, photos that disappeared when a re-download failed, sections that tied for the same position and shuffled, and a duplicate hotel left behind by an earlier run. This was the pass that turned a working tool into one a salesperson can put in front of a prospect.

Admin panelNevron ops

Re-reading a hotel's website stopped trampling the hotel's own work

A hotel built from its website can be refreshed when that website changes. The danger is obvious: the hotel has since edited its menus by hand, and a refresh must not wipe that out. The rule is now explicit — the update never destroys what it did not create. Content the reader originally made is replaced cleanly, content the hotel added itself is left alone, and a section that has simply been renamed on the website is recognised as the same section rather than duplicated beside the old one. The admin panel also learned to label this content as coming from the website, so a hotelier can see at a glance which parts arrived automatically.

Guest app

Opening the app got quicker, and staying in it got easier

Guest app

Guests can look back at their previous stays

The guest app used to show only the stay happening right now. Everything before it simply vanished. Guests now have a Past stays view listing the visits they have made, so returning guests can look back at where they stayed and what they did — and the app stops behaving as though each visit were the first. Usage of the phone apps is measured alongside it, so Nevron can see how much of the guest app is actually reached from a downloaded app rather than a browser.

PR #862
Guest appCrew app

The app stopped signing guests out when it was simply offline

Opening the app with no signal, or after it had been closed for a while, could log a guest straight out — a full re-login for what was really just a missing connection. That is fixed. A cold start now keeps the guest signed in, the app resumes in the hotel they were last in, and the list of hotel options survives offline instead of coming up empty. Both the guest app and the crew app also offer the saved email address on the sign-in screen, so the phone's own autofill works.

Faster to open, and no reload when you change language

Starting the app used to fetch what it needed one thing after another; those requests now happen at the same time, so the first screen arrives sooner. The hotel list and the menus around it were tidied up in the same pass, and a shared item link now returns to the app properly instead of dropping the guest on an error page.

Languages

Getting every last English word out of a guest's screen

Guest appWidget

The last English words in the guest's app were hunted down

A guest reading the app in Slovene still met English in the awkward places: the suggestion pills in the chat, the assistant's greeting, the word “Uncategorized”, the status line under a conversation, and — most visibly — the names of the items already in their basket. All of those now follow the guest's chosen language. The fixes are individually small and collectively the difference between an app that is translated and an app that feels translated.

PR #870PR #915PR #894
Admin panelNevron ops

A new hotel now knows which language its own content is in

When a hotel is built from its website, the text comes in whatever language that website is written in. The platform used to treat it all as though it were English, which made every later translation slightly wrong. Provisioning now records the content's real language, pins the original text as that language, and keeps English as the base to translate from. The platform's own language list was cleaned up at the same time — plain, standard language codes, a curated set rather than an accumulating pile — and the standard room-care menus now ship with proper Slovene and Croatian wording instead of machine output.

PR #886PR #935PR #936PR #923
Admin panel

Hotels keep their own wording, and the platform stops re-translating it

Hotels can override the platform's standard wording with their own. The translation machinery did not fully respect that: it could re-translate a phrase a hotel had already settled on, and in some cases translate the same language twice over. The system now distinguishes clearly between wording a hotel owns, wording it inherits from the platform, and wording that is genuinely missing — and only ever works on the last of those.

In-room TV & signage

Screens that keep playing the right thing

In-room TVSignage

Signage screens play what they were told, and notice when it changes

Lobby and hallway screens had a cluster of problems that all looked like “the screen is showing the wrong thing”: playback, transitions and exiting a playlist did not behave as configured, a playlist that had been assigned to nobody still targeted something, and a screen went on playing its old playlist after the schedule or its device group had been changed — until someone physically restarted it. Screens now pick up changes by themselves.

PR #927PR #899
In-room TV

Televisions stopped being mislabelled and wrongly restarted

Two long-standing television irritations were cleared. A TV's network hardware address was being stored and displayed as though it were an Android device identifier, which made the device list misleading to anyone trying to match a screen to a room. And the watchdog that is supposed to rescue a stalled live channel was tearing down healthy playback on LG sets — turning working television off in the name of fixing it.

Under the hood

Capacity, safety, and things that must not leak

Infrastructure

On-premise installations stopped shipping with open storage

Some hotels run the platform on their own hardware rather than in Nevron's cloud. Those installations were being set up with the file storage on default credentials and publicly reachable ports — a door left unlocked on someone else's premises. Each installation now generates its own credentials and keeps the storage ports private to the machine.

PR #858
Infrastructure

Every outgoing email is now checked for which hotel it belongs to

In a platform where every hotel's data is kept separate, an email is the easiest place for that separation to slip — a background job sends a message without carrying the hotel's context, and the content is fetched from whichever hotel happened to be in scope. Every send is now guarded by an explicit hotel check, and an old unused sending path was deleted rather than left to rot.

PR #843
Guest appInfrastructure

The AI assistant got more headroom, and always answers

The assistant's per-guest reply limit was low enough that a guest in a genuine back-and-forth could hit it — being throttled for the crime of having a conversation. The limit went from 5 to 15 replies a minute and the machines handling AI chat were given more capacity. Separately, the assistant sometimes replied with item cards and no words at all, which reads as the assistant ignoring the question; it now always writes an answer alongside whatever it shows.
