Everything the platform does, finally written down

Nevron now sells a platform with dozens of separate capabilities spread across five different screens — the guest app, the in-room TV, the admin panel, the crew app and the website widget. Until this fortnight there was no single place that described them all. A new salesperson had to learn the product by asking colleagues, and a question like “does Nevron do that?” was answered from memory. This cycle produced the answer as a real, maintained product.

Fifty-five capabilities were written up and approved, in English and Slovene. Each one says what it is, who uses it, how it works step by step, what it is worth to a hotel in hotel terms — and, deliberately, what the hotel can configure versus what is fixed. That last line exists to stop reps over-promising: because every hotel is set up differently, “does it do X?” is often genuinely ambiguous, and now the honest answer is written down.

It is not a slide deck. The catalogue lives inside the admin panel as a searchable page with screenshots, filtered by guest-journey stage, by screen, or by who uses it, with a NEW badge that rolls off by itself after 90 days. A new viewer login opens that page and nothing else — safe to hand to a salesperson who should never see a real hotel's data. Every screenshot is captured from a purpose-built demo hotel, so no real guest ever appears in a sales conversation. The same content also ships as one plain-text file per language for the sales team's own AI assistant to answer from.

Guest app

The app got faster, friendlier, and harder to get locked out of

Guest app

Photos stopped being the slowest thing in the app

The guest app was downloading full-size photographs to fill thumbnails the size of a postage stamp. Now the platform publishes small and medium versions of every picture, each screen asks for the size it will actually show, photos arrive straight from the content delivery network instead of detouring through our servers, and pictures that do not need perfect fidelity stop shipping lossless. Opening an item is quicker — and it opens on the photograph you tapped, instead of flashing a different one first.

Guest app

A finished stay no longer shuts the door behind you

When a guest's stay ended, the app treated them as a stranger: the hotel became unreachable and they hit a dead end with no way forward. Now a past stay is labelled as ended, and instead of an error the guest sees a short explanation and is taken into the hotel as a visitor — still able to look around, without the parts of the app that belong to a current stay.

Turning notifications on is now something a guest can find

Phone notifications worked, but there was no obvious switch to turn them on — and when they did arrive they were written in English, wore a blank grey icon on Android, and dropped you on the app's front page. All four are fixed: there is a visible toggle in the account screen and an offer in the notification bell, the text arrives in the recipient's language, the Nevron mark shows in the status bar, and tapping an order notification opens My Orders.

Languages

The platform learned to speak to a person, not to a hotel

Guest app

A guest's language now follows the guest

Language used to belong to the hotel, and to whichever screen you happened to be looking at. Now it belongs to the account: a guest sets it once, and everything addressed to them personally — emails, phone notifications, the app itself — arrives in that language, at every hotel they ever stay in.

The crew app speaks the crew's language

Hotel crews are rarely monolingual, and the crew app was. It now has a language picker, and work alerts follow it: a task assignment or an escalation reaches each person in the language they chose, whether it comes as a phone notification or as an email.

Reliability & safety

Things that must not fail quietly

Under the hood

The night shift stopped falling asleep

The platform's scheduler — the part that runs everything nobody presses a button for, from TV guide imports to nightly cleanups — could freeze silently in production. Nothing crashed, nothing alerted; scheduled work simply stopped happening until someone noticed days later. The cause was traced to a fault in a third-party library, which is now pinned to a safe version, and the connections were given proper timeouts so a dead link fails loudly instead of hanging forever.

Under the hoodIn QA

Five doors that should never have opened by a side entrance

Some parts of the platform belong to no single hotel — the payment provider's callback, for example. It was possible to reach those through a hotel-prefixed address, which is a shape of request that should never have been accepted. Five such entrances were found and closed, and the rule is now enforced centrally rather than remembered per endpoint.

PR #777PR #789
Integrations

The hotel-system link stopped deleting on incomplete evidence

When the platform syncs with a hotel's own reservation system, it removes stays that have disappeared on the hotel's side. If a sync was cut short, though, the missing half looked identical to deleted bookings — and the platform believed it. Now a clean-up only runs when the hotel's system has proved it sent everything.

PR #752
Under the hoodIn QA

Nothing in storage outlives the hotel it belonged to

Files kept accumulating: images left behind by deleted hotels, export archives nobody collected, photos pointing at folders that no longer exist. A lifecycle sweep now cleans all three up — orphaned files are found and removed, old export archives expire, and files pointing at the wrong folder are repaired instead of silently breaking.

Faster to build on, safer to change

Under the hood

Two email systems became one

The platform had been sending email through two different engines at once — an old one and its replacement — which meant every new kind of message had to be built twice, and it was never obvious which one a given email used. The old engine, 1,300 lines of it, has now been deleted, with every remaining message type moved across.

PR #824PR #808PR #783PR #791
Under the hoodIn QA

Tests that copy a hotel instead of building one

Automated tests constantly need a fresh hotel to work in, and each one was being built from scratch by replaying roughly 470 setup steps — about 55 seconds per hotel, thousands of times a week. Tests now restore a pre-built master copy instead: about 7.5 seconds, some seven times faster, with the copy rebuilding itself automatically whenever the underlying structure changes.

Under the hood

The nightly check-through learned to fix itself

Every night the platform clicks through itself — signing in, ordering, browsing — to catch anything that broke during the day. The weakness was that these runs drifted red for boring reasons and then got ignored. There is now a nightly triage that decides, for each new failure, whether the test environment drifted (heal it and re-run), the test itself is at fault (prepare a fix overnight, never merged unreviewed), or the product genuinely broke (diagnose it and file it) — and leaves a plain-language report each morning.
